Module 01

Fundamentals and Unit Conversion

Core formulas

Let:

  • $S$ = speed
  • $D$ = distance
  • $T$ = time

The three equivalent fundamental kinematics relations are:

📐The Triad of Kinematics
$$ \Large S = \frac{D}{T} \qquad\qquad D = S \times T \qquad\qquad T = \frac{D}{S} $$

Unit conversion

  • km/h to m/s: multiply by $\Large \frac{5}{18}$.
  • m/s to km/h: multiply by $\Large \frac{18}{5}$.
⚡Direct Unit Conversion Relations
$$ \Large \text{Speed in m/s} = \text{Speed in km/h} \times \frac{5}{18} $$
$$ \Large \text{Speed in km/h} = \text{Speed in m/s} \times \frac{18}{5} $$
💡Why does this conversion factor work?

1 kilometre has $1000\text{ metres}$, and 1 hour has $3600\text{ seconds}$ ($60 \times 60$):

$$ \Large 1\text{ km/h} = \frac{1000\text{ m}}{3600\text{ s}} = \frac{10}{36}\text{ m/s} = \frac{5}{18}\text{ m/s} $$

Inverting the ratio yields $1\text{ m/s} = \frac{18}{5}\text{ km/h} = 3.6\text{ km/h}$.

Warm-up examples

Example 1.1 • Unit Conversion

Convert $180\text{ km/h}$ to m/s.

Express a vehicle velocity of $180\text{ km/h}$ into metres per second ($\text{m/s}$).
Step 1: Apply the $\frac{5}{18}$ conversion multiplier
$$ \Large 180 \times \frac{5}{18} = (180 \div 18) \times 5 = 10 \times 5 = 50\text{ m/s} $$
✓ Final Answer: $$\mathbf{50\text{ m/s}}$$
Example 1.2 • Unit Conversion

A car covers $7.5\text{ m}$ in $1\text{ s}$. Find its speed in km/h.

A motorcar travels a distance of $7.5\text{ metres}$ in exactly $1\text{ second}$. Calculate its speed expressed in kilometres per hour ($\text{km/h}$).
Step 1: Compute speed in m/s
$$ \Large S = \frac{D}{T} = \frac{7.5\text{ m}}{1\text{ s}} = 7.5\text{ m/s} $$
Step 2: Multiply by $\frac{18}{5}$ to convert to km/h
$$ \Large 7.5 \times \frac{18}{5} = \frac{15}{2} \times \frac{18}{5} = 3 \times 9 = 27\text{ km/h} $$

Alternatively: $7.5 \div 5 = 1.5$, then $1.5 \times 18 = 27\text{ km/h}$.

✓ Final Answer: $$\mathbf{27\text{ km/h}}$$
✍️Active Checkpoint 1.1
A bullet train travels at $126\text{ km/h}$. What distance does it cover in $14\text{ seconds}$?
Hint: First convert $126\text{ km/h}$ to $\text{m/s}$ using $\frac{5}{18}$. Notice that $126 = 18 \times 7$. Then multiply speed in $\text{m/s}$ by $14\text{ s}$.

Step 1: Convert speed: $126 \times \frac{5}{18} = 7 \times 5 = 35\text{ m/s}$.

Step 2: Distance covered: $D = S \times T = 35\text{ m/s} \times 14\text{ s} = \mathbf{490\text{ m}}$.

Module 02

Trains Crossing Stationary Objects

Type 1A: A point object (person, pole, or tree)

A point object has negligible physical length along the track. The train must travel only its own length for its rear end to completely clear the object.

Let $L$ be the train's length and $S_{\text{train}}$ be the train's speed:

📐Crossing a Point Object
$$ \Large \boxed{D = L} \qquad\qquad \boxed{T = \frac{L}{S_{\text{train}}}} $$
💡Key Idea

Never add another length for a person, pole, or tree. Their physical thickness is negligible compared with the train length.

Type 1B: An extended object (platform, bridge, or tunnel)

The entire train must clear the object from engine tip to caboose rear. Therefore, the train must travel a total distance equal to the sum of both lengths.

📐Crossing an Extended Object
$$ \Large \boxed{D = L_{\text{train}} + L_{\text{object}}} $$
$$ \Large \boxed{T = \frac{L_{\text{train}} + L_{\text{object}}}{S_{\text{train}}}} $$
⚠️Unit Consistency Reminder

Ensure that the speed is converted to m/s when distance is given in metres and crossing time is required in seconds!

Worked examples

Example 2.1 • Platform Crossing

Train crossing a platform

A platform is $248\text{ m}$ long. A train of length $127\text{ m}$ travels at $150\text{ km/h}$. How many seconds does it take to clear the platform?
Step 1: Total distance to clear
$$ \Large D = L_{\text{platform}} + L_{\text{train}} = 248 + 127 = 375\text{ m} $$
Step 2: Convert speed to m/s
$$ \Large S = 150 \times \frac{5}{18} = \frac{150 \times 5}{18} = \frac{25 \times 5}{3} = \frac{125}{3}\text{ m/s} $$
Step 3: Calculate clearing time
$$ \Large T = \frac{D}{S} = \frac{375}{125/3} = 375 \times \frac{3}{125} = 3 \times 3 = \boxed{9\text{ seconds}} $$
✓ Final Answer: $$\mathbf{9\text{ seconds}}$$
Example 2.2 • Equal Lengths

Train and platform have equal lengths

A train travels at $72\text{ km/h}$ and crosses a platform of equal length in $40\text{ s}$. Find the train's length.
Step 1: Assign variables and convert speed

Let the train and platform each have length $L$ metres.

$$ \Large S = 72 \times \frac{5}{18} = 4 \times 5 = 20\text{ m/s} $$
Step 2: Total distance covered
$$ \Large D = S \times T = 20\text{ m/s} \times 40\text{ s} = 800\text{ m} $$
Step 3: Solve for $L$
$$ \Large D = L_{\text{train}} + L_{\text{platform}} = L + L = 2L $$
$$ \Large 2L = 800 \implies \boxed{L = 400\text{ m}} $$
✓ Final Answer: $$\mathbf{L = 400\text{ m}}$$
✍️Active Checkpoint 2.1
A train of length $200\text{ m}$ crosses an electric pole in $10\text{ seconds}$. How long will it take to cross a platform $300\text{ m}$ long at the same speed?
Hint: First find speed from pole: $S = 200 / 10 = 20\text{ m/s}$. Then for platform, total distance is $200 + 300 = 500\text{ m}$.

Speed: $S = 200\text{ m} / 10\text{ s} = 20\text{ m/s}$.

Platform crossing: $T = (200 + 300) / 20 = 500 / 20 = \mathbf{25\text{ seconds}}$.

Module 03

Relative Speed Framework

When two bodies move at the same time, their separation distance is governed by their relative speed ($S_{\text{relative}}$). This single framework unifies train crossings, police-thief pursuits, and rendezvous problems.

📐The Master Relative Motion Formula
$$ \Large \boxed{T = \frac{\text{Gap}}{S_{\text{relative}}}} $$
Direction of Movement Relative Speed Physical Rationale
Same Direction (Chase / Overtake) $$\Large \lvert S_1 - S_2\rvert$$ The faster body closes (or widens) the separation gap only at the difference between their individual speeds.
Opposite Directions (Moving towards each other) $$\Large S_1 + S_2$$ Both bodies move simultaneously toward each other, shrinking the separation gap at the sum of their speeds.
⚠️Crucial Unit Reminder

Convert both speeds to the exact same unit (both in $\text{m/s}$ or both in $\text{km/h}$) before adding or subtracting them! Never add a speed in $\text{km/h}$ to a speed in $\text{m/s}$.

Module 04

Chasing Problems

Classical aptitude chase problems include a policeman chasing a thief, one vehicle overtaking another, or a faster runner catching up with a slower runner given a head start.

For two bodies moving in the same direction, where the chaser is faster ($S_{\text{chaser}} > S_{\text{runner}}$):

📐Time to Catch & Distance Run
$$ \Large \boxed{T_{\text{catch}} = \frac{\text{Initial gap}}{S_{\text{chaser}} - S_{\text{runner}}}} $$
$$ \Large \boxed{D_{\text{chaser}} = S_{\text{chaser}} \times T_{\text{catch}}} $$

Worked examples

Example 4.1 • Policeman & Thief

Policeman and thief pursuit

A policeman spots a thief $100\text{ m}$ ahead. The policeman runs at $4\text{ m/s}$, while the thief runs at $2\text{ m/s}$. Find the distance the policeman runs before catching the thief.
Step 1: Compute relative speed
$$ \Large S_r = S_{\text{police}} - S_{\text{thief}} = 4 - 2 = 2\text{ m/s} $$
Step 2: Time required to close the 100m gap
$$ \Large T_{\text{catch}} = \frac{\text{Initial gap}}{S_r} = \frac{100}{2} = 50\text{ s} $$
Step 3: Distance covered by the policeman
$$ \Large D = S_{\text{police}} \times T_{\text{catch}} = 4\text{ m/s} \times 50\text{ s} = \boxed{200\text{ m}} $$

(Note: During these 50 seconds, the thief also covers $2 \times 50 = 100\text{ m}$. Initial $100\text{ m} + 100\text{ m} = 200\text{ m}$, perfectly matching!)

✓ Final Answer: $$\mathbf{200\text{ m}}$$
Example 4.2 • Carriage Overtaking

Carriage overtaking a walking pedestrian

A man walks at $3\text{ km/h}$. A carriage overtakes him and remains visible up to a distance of $100\text{ m}$ for $2\text{ minutes}$. Find the carriage's speed.
Step 1: Convert elapsed time to seconds
$$ \Large 2\text{ minutes} = 2 \times 60 = 120\text{ s} $$
Step 2: Relative speed at which separation expands

The gap expands by $100\text{ m}$ in $120\text{ s}$:

$$ \Large S_r = \frac{100}{120} = \frac{5}{6}\text{ m/s} $$
Step 3: Convert relative speed to km/h
$$ \Large S_r = \frac{5}{6} \times \frac{18}{5} = \frac{18}{6} = 3\text{ km/h} $$
Step 4: Solve for carriage speed

Since both travel in the same direction:

$$ \Large S_{\text{carriage}} - S_{\text{man}} = S_r $$
$$ \Large S_{\text{carriage}} - 3 = 3 \implies \boxed{S_{\text{carriage}} = 6\text{ km/h}} $$
✓ Final Answer: $$\mathbf{6\text{ km/h}}$$
Example 4.3 • Bus & Truck

Bus catching a truck

A bus travels at $45\text{ km/h}$ and catches a truck $150\text{ m}$ ahead in $30\text{ s}$. Find the truck's speed.
Step 1: Compute relative speed in m/s
$$ \Large S_r = \frac{\text{Gap}}{\text{Time}} = \frac{150\text{ m}}{30\text{ s}} = 5\text{ m/s} $$
Step 2: Convert relative speed to km/h
$$ \Large S_r = 5 \times \frac{18}{5} = 18\text{ km/h} $$
Step 3: Solve for truck speed
$$ \Large S_{\text{bus}} - S_{\text{truck}} = S_r $$
$$ \Large 45 - S_{\text{truck}} = 18 \implies S_{\text{truck}} = 45 - 18 = \boxed{27\text{ km/h}} $$
✓ Final Answer: $$\mathbf{27\text{ km/h}}$$

Module 05

Meeting Point Problems

When two people, vehicles, or trains start from two separated points and move directly towards each other, they work together to eliminate the initial distance. Their speeds add up.

📐Time to Rendezvous
$$ \Large \boxed{T_{\text{meet}} = \frac{\text{Initial distance between them}}{S_1 + S_2}} $$

Worked example

Example 5.1 • Two People Moving Towards Each Other

Two people moving towards each other

Two people are $19\text{ km}$ apart and move towards each other at $21\text{ km/h}$ and $17\text{ km/h}$. When will they meet?
Step 1: Compute combined relative speed
$$ \Large S_r = S_1 + S_2 = 21 + 17 = 38\text{ km/h} $$
Step 2: Calculate meeting time in hours
$$ \Large T = \frac{19\text{ km}}{38\text{ km/h}} = \frac{1}{2}\text{ hour} $$
Step 3: Convert to minutes
$$ \Large T = \frac{1}{2} \times 60 = \boxed{30\text{ minutes}} $$
✓ Final Answer: $$\mathbf{30\text{ minutes}}$$

Module 06

Trains Crossing Moving Objects

Crossing a moving person

A moving person has negligible length. The train must cover only its own length relative to the moving person.

📐Train Crossing Moving Person
$$ \Large \boxed{T = \frac{L_{\text{train}}}{S_{\text{relative}}}} $$

Crossing another train

Both trains possess physical lengths. For them to clear each other completely from first contact to last bumper, the total relative distance covered is the sum of their lengths.

📐Two Trains Crossing
$$ \Large \boxed{D = L_1 + L_2} $$
$$ \Large \boxed{T = \frac{L_1 + L_2}{S_{\text{relative}}}} $$

Relative speed selection:

  • Same direction: $S_{\text{relative}} = \lvert S_1 - S_2\rvert$
  • Opposite directions: $S_{\text{relative}} = S_1 + S_2$

Worked example

Example 6.1 • Two Trains in Opposite Directions

Two trains moving towards each other

Two trains of lengths $121\text{ m}$ and $99\text{ m}$ travel in opposite directions at $40\text{ km/h}$ and $32\text{ km/h}$. Find the time they take to cross each other completely.
Step 1: Total distance to clear each other
$$ \Large D = L_1 + L_2 = 121 + 99 = 220\text{ m} $$
Step 2: Relative speed (opposite directions add)
$$ \Large S_r = 40 + 32 = 72\text{ km/h} $$
$$ \Large S_r = 72 \times \frac{5}{18} = 4 \times 5 = 20\text{ m/s} $$
Step 3: Calculate crossing time
$$ \Large T = \frac{D}{S_r} = \frac{220\text{ m}}{20\text{ m/s}} = \boxed{11\text{ seconds}} $$
✓ Final Answer: $$\mathbf{11\text{ seconds}}$$

Module 07

Logical Differential Method (Two Crossings)

This is one of the highest-value shortcuts in campus placement tests. When the same train crosses two different objects (such as a person and then a platform, or two platforms of different lengths), you do not need to set up two simultaneous algebraic equations with $L$ and $S$.

Worked example

Example 7.1 • Differential Shortcut

Train crossing a standing person and then a platform

A train crosses a standing person in $20\text{ s}$ and a platform $135\text{ m}$ long in $29\text{ s}$. Find the train's speed.
Logical Reasoning:
  • Crossing a person takes $20\text{ s}$ to cover the train's own length ($L$).
  • Crossing the platform takes $29\text{ s}$ to cover the train's length plus $135\text{ m}$ ($L + 135$).
  • The extra time is $29 - 20 = 9\text{ s}$. That extra time accounts solely for the platform's $135\text{ m}$.
Calculate speed directly:
$$ \Large S = \frac{\text{Extra distance}}{\text{Extra time}} = \frac{135}{29 - 20} = \frac{135}{9} = 15\text{ m/s} $$
Convert to km/h:
$$ \Large S = 15 \times \frac{18}{5} = 3 \times 18 = \boxed{54\text{ km/h}} $$
Bonus deduction: Find the train's length ($L$)

Since the train crosses a standing person ($D = L$) in $20\text{ s}$:

$$ \Large L = S \times T = 15\text{ m/s} \times 20\text{ s} = 300\text{ m} $$
✓ Final Answer: $$\mathbf{54\text{ km/h}}\quad (\text{Train Length} = 300\text{ m})$$
⚡The Master Differential Shortcut Formula
$$ \Large \boxed{S = \frac{\text{Difference in object lengths}}{\text{Difference in crossing times}}} $$

Applies whenever the same train travels at constant speed across two objects where differences in distances and times are known. (When one object is a pole/person, its length is $0$).

Module 08

Ratio and Proportionality

Case A: Constant time ($T$ is fixed)

When two journeys or entities travel for the exact same amount of elapsed time, distance covered is directly proportional to speed:

📐Constant Time Proportionality
$$ \Large \boxed{\frac{D_1}{D_2} = \frac{S_1}{S_2}} $$

Example: Out-and-back meeting point

Example 8.1 • Constant Time Meeting

Rohit and Mohit on a line track

P and Q are $42\text{ km}$ apart. Rohit and Mohit start at P and travel towards Q at $6\text{ km/h}$ and $8\text{ km/h}$, respectively. Mohit reaches Q, immediately turns back, and meets Rohit at R. Find $PR$.
Method 1: Proportionality Equation

Let $QR = x\text{ km}$.

  • Mohit's distance: $PQ + QR = 42 + x$
  • Rohit's distance: $PQ - QR = 42 - x$

Since both start simultaneously and meet at R, elapsed time is identical:

$$ \Large \frac{42 + x}{42 - x} = \frac{8}{6} = \frac{4}{3} $$

Cross-multiply:

$$ \Large 3(42 + x) = 4(42 - x) $$
$$ \Large 126 + 3x = 168 - 4x $$
$$ \Large 7x = 42 \implies x = 6\text{ km} $$
$$ \Large PR = 42 - x = 42 - 6 = \boxed{36\text{ km}} $$
Method 2: 10-Second Combined Distance Shortcut

Together, Mohit and Rohit have covered twice the total distance between P and Q:

$$ \Large D_{\text{total}} = 2 \times 42 = 84\text{ km} $$

Combined speed = $6 + 8 = 14\text{ km/h}$. Elapsed time = $\frac{84}{14} = 6\text{ hours}$.

Rohit covers distance $PR$: $D_{\text{Rohit}} = 6\text{ km/h} \times 6\text{ h} = \mathbf{36\text{ km}}$.

✓ Final Answer: $$\mathbf{PR = 36\text{ km}}$$

Case B: Constant distance ($D$ is fixed)

For a fixed journey distance, speed and time are inversely proportional. Higher speed directly reduces journey time by the reciprocal ratio.

📐Inverse Proportionality
$$ \Large \boxed{S \propto \frac{1}{T}} \qquad\qquad \boxed{\frac{T_1}{T_2} = \frac{S_2}{S_1}} $$

Example: Reduced speed and lateness

Example 8.2 • Lateness Problem

Walking at reduced speed

A student walks at $\frac{3}{4}$ of their usual speed and arrives $12\text{ minutes}$ late. Find the usual travel time.
Step 1: Write the speed ratio
$$ \Large S_{\text{usual}} : S_{\text{new}} = 1 : \frac{3}{4} = 4 : 3 $$
Step 2: Invert to obtain the time ratio

Since distance is fixed, time ratio is the inverse of speed ratio:

$$ \Large T_{\text{usual}} : T_{\text{new}} = 3 : 4 $$
Step 3: Relate ratio difference to given time

The difference between new time and usual time is $4 - 3 = 1\text{ ratio unit}$.

$$ \Large 1\text{ ratio unit} = 12\text{ minutes} $$
$$ \Large T_{\text{usual}} = 3 \times 12 = \boxed{36\text{ minutes}} $$

(And the new delayed journey takes $4 \times 12 = 48\text{ minutes}$, which is indeed $48 - 36 = 12\text{ minutes}$ late!)

✓ Final Answer: $$\mathbf{36\text{ minutes}}$$

Module 09

Average Speed

Universal formula

Always hold firm to the universal definition:

📐Universal Definition of Average Speed
$$ \Large \boxed{\text{Average speed} = \frac{\text{Total distance}}{\text{Total time}}} $$
🚫Critical Placement Trap

Do NOT use $\Large \frac{x + y}{2}$ for equal-distance trips! An arithmetic mean assumes equal time spent at each speed. When distances are equal, more time is spent moving at the slower speed, dragging the true average speed down.

Special case 1: Equal distances ($D_1 = D_2$)

If a person travels a distance at speed $x$ and returns over the same distance at speed $y$, the average speed is given by the harmonic mean:

📐Equal Distances Average Speed
$$ \Large \boxed{S_{\text{avg}} = \frac{2xy}{x + y}} $$
Example 9.1 • Equal Distance Round Trip

Travel at $20\text{ km/h}$ and return at $30\text{ km/h}$

A traveler journeys to a destination at $20\text{ km/h}$ and returns over the exact same distance at $30\text{ km/h}$. Find the average speed.
Calculation:
$$ \Large S_{\text{avg}} = \frac{2 \times 20 \times 30}{20 + 30} = \frac{1200}{50} = \boxed{24\text{ km/h}} $$

The average is not $25\text{ km/h}$, because the slower leg ($20\text{ km/h}$) takes $50\%$ more time than the faster leg ($30\text{ km/h}$).

✓ Final Answer: $$\mathbf{24\text{ km/h}}$$

Special case 2: Equal time durations ($T_1 = T_2$)

If a person travels at speed $x$ for duration $t$, and then at speed $y$ for the exact same duration $t$:

📐Equal Times Average Speed
$$ \Large \boxed{S_{\text{avg}} = \frac{x + y}{2}} $$

Combined exam examples

Example 9.2 • Equal Distances

Car: Delhi to Chandigarh and back

The car travels at $120\text{ km/h}$ on the onward journey and $80\text{ km/h}$ on the return journey. The distances are equal. Find the average speed.
Solution:
$$ \Large S_{\text{avg}} = \frac{2 \times 120 \times 80}{120 + 80} = \frac{19200}{200} = \boxed{96\text{ km/h}} $$
✓ Final Answer: $$\mathbf{96\text{ km/h}}$$
Example 9.3 • Equal Durations

Train: Two journeys of equal duration

A train travels at $60\text{ km/h}$ for time $t$, then at $40\text{ km/h}$ for the same time $t$. Find the average speed.
Solution:

Since the time intervals are identical, the simple arithmetic mean applies:

$$ \Large S_{\text{avg}} = \frac{60 + 40}{2} = \boxed{50\text{ km/h}} $$
✓ Final Answer: $$\mathbf{50\text{ km/h}}$$

Module 10

Boats and Streams

Let:

  • $B$ = speed of the boat (or swimmer) in still water
  • $W$ = speed of the stream / water current
  • $D$ = downstream speed (with the current)
  • $U$ = upstream speed (against the current)

Downstream and upstream formulas

Downstream: The current flows in the same direction, pushing the boat faster:

🌊Downstream Speed
$$ \Large \boxed{D = B + W} $$

Upstream: The current opposes the boat's motion:

⛰️Upstream Speed
$$ \Large \boxed{U = B - W} $$

Standard shortcuts

Adding the two equations yields boat speed in still water:

⛵Still Water Boat Speed
$$ \Large \boxed{B = \frac{D + U}{2}} $$

Subtracting the two equations yields stream current speed:

💧Stream Current Speed
$$ \Large \boxed{W = \frac{D - U}{2}} $$

Worked examples

Example 10.1 • Stream Current

Find the speed of the current

A boat covers $100\text{ km}$ downstream in $10\text{ h}$ and $75\text{ km}$ upstream in $15\text{ h}$. Find the current speed.
Step 1: Compute downstream and upstream speeds
$$ \Large D = \frac{100\text{ km}}{10\text{ h}} = 10\text{ km/h} $$
$$ \Large U = \frac{75\text{ km}}{15\text{ h}} = 5\text{ km/h} $$
Step 2: Calculate stream speed ($W$)
$$ \Large W = \frac{D - U}{2} = \frac{10 - 5}{2} = \frac{5}{2} = \boxed{2.5\text{ km/h}} $$
Bonus: Boat speed in still water ($B$)
$$ \Large B = \frac{D + U}{2} = \frac{10 + 5}{2} = \frac{15}{2} = \boxed{7.5\text{ km/h}} $$
✓ Final Answer: $$\mathbf{W = 2.5\text{ km/h}}\quad (B = 7.5\text{ km/h})$$
Example 10.2 • Still Water Speed

Find the speed of the person/boat in still water

A man travels $10\text{ km}$ upstream in $8\text{ h}$ and $36\text{ km}$ downstream in $6\text{ h}$. Find his speed in still water.
Step 1: Compute upstream and downstream speeds
$$ \Large U = \frac{10\text{ km}}{8\text{ h}} = 1.25\text{ km/h} $$
$$ \Large D = \frac{36\text{ km}}{6\text{ h}} = 6\text{ km/h} $$
Step 2: Calculate still-water speed ($B$)
$$ \Large B = \frac{D + U}{2} = \frac{6 + 1.25}{2} = \frac{7.25}{2} = \boxed{3.625\text{ km/h}} $$

(And stream current speed $W = \frac{6 - 1.25}{2} = \frac{4.75}{2} = 2.375\text{ km/h}$)

✓ Final Answer: $$\mathbf{B = 3.625\text{ km/h}}$$

Module 11

Placement Strategy and Common Mistakes

Why these shortcuts matter

In timed aptitude rounds (such as TCS NQT, Cognizant, or campus tests), average time per question is typically 60 to 90 seconds. Building long two-variable equations from scratch wastes critical minutes and increases algebraic calculation errors. Practise recognizing the question type before putting pen to paper.

What to practise & checklist

📋The 5-Point Exam Checklist
  1. Memorize the conversion factors:
    $$ \Large \boxed{\text{km/h} \times \frac{5}{18} = \text{m/s}} \qquad\qquad \boxed{\text{m/s} \times \frac{18}{5} = \text{km/h}} $$
  2. Check the object geometry: For train questions, immediately check whether the object is a point object ($D = L$) or has physical length ($D = L + L_{\text{object}}$).
  3. Determine motion direction: For relative motion, identify direction first. Same direction $\implies \lvert S_1 - S_2\rvert$; opposite directions $\implies S_1 + S_2$.
  4. Boats and streams routine: Calculate $D$ and $U$ first, then immediately use: $$\Large B = \frac{D + U}{2}, \qquad W = \frac{D - U}{2}$$
  5. Average speed check: Verify whether the journey has equal distances ($\frac{2xy}{x+y}$) or equal time durations ($\frac{x+y}{2}$).

Common mistakes to avoid

🚫Top 5 Avoidable Exam Pitfalls
  • Mixing units: Mixing $\text{m/s}$ and $\text{km/h}$ while adding or subtracting speeds.
  • Forgetting object lengths: Forgetting to add train and platform lengths when crossing an extended object.
  • Wrong sign for same direction: Using the sum of speeds when two bodies move in the same direction.
  • Arithmetic mean trap: Using $\frac{x + y}{2}$ for an equal-distance round trip instead of the harmonic mean.
  • Confusing downstream and upstream: Downstream is faster ($B + W$) because the water current aids the boat; upstream is slower ($B - W$).

Practice Problem: Two Bridges

Featured Placement Problem

Train crossing two bridges of different lengths

A train crosses two bridges of lengths $800\text{ m}$ and $400\text{ m}$ in $100\text{ s}$ and $60\text{ s}$, respectively. Find the length and speed of the train.
Method 1: Step-by-Step Algebraic Solution

Let:

  • $L$ = train length in metres
  • $S$ = train speed in $\text{m/s}$

For the $800\text{ m}$ bridge:

$$ \Large \frac{L + 800}{S} = 100 \implies L + 800 = 100S \tag{1} $$

For the $400\text{ m}$ bridge:

$$ \Large \frac{L + 400}{S} = 60 \implies L + 400 = 60S \tag{2} $$

Subtract equation (2) from equation (1):

$$ \Large (L + 800) - (L + 400) = 100S - 60S $$
$$ \Large 400 = 40S \implies \boxed{S = 10\text{ m/s}} $$

Convert speed to km/h:

$$ \Large S = 10 \times \frac{18}{5} = \boxed{36\text{ km/h}} $$

Substitute $S = 10\text{ m/s}$ back into equation (2):

$$ \Large L + 400 = 60 \times 10 = 600 \implies \boxed{L = 200\text{ m}} $$
Method 2: Faster Logical Shortcut (Exam Speed)

Difference between bridge lengths = $800 - 400 = 400\text{ m}$.

Difference between crossing times = $100 - 60 = 40\text{ s}$.

$$ \Large S = \frac{400\text{ m}}{40\text{ s}} = 10\text{ m/s} = 36\text{ km/h} $$

Then train length:

$$ \Large L = (S \times 60) - 400 = (10 \times 60) - 400 = 600 - 400 = \mathbf{200\text{ m}} $$
✓ Final Answer: $$\mathbf{\text{Train Length} = 200\text{ m}},\quad \mathbf{\text{Speed} = 10\text{ m/s} = 36\text{ km/h}}$$
